Courtyard

A Two-Player VR Experience for Height Exposure

Aimed to provide a height-exposure environment for patients afflicted with a fear of heights. Therapist and patient explore an apartment building that constitutes a stage for various tasks designed to promote exposure. Tasks can be freely assigned to any floor, enabling the modification of difficulty levels.

In this multi-user, co-located Unity VR project, I have developed a task assignment system that allows users to assign their chosen task to a specific floor in an apartment building. I have implemented a fully functional elevator, which can be used by all users simultaneously to reach any floor in the apartment. In addition to developing the whole project, I have created some of the assets found in this project using Blender for 3D modeling and rigging, and Adobe Substance Painter for texturing.

A core feature of this project is the selection of tasks designed to bring the patient close to the edge of the balconies. I have developed all tasks, some of which include repairing a sattelite dish, cleaning windows, planting seeds, walking on a plank and more. I have populated the floors mostly with assets available online, and some assets created by me.

Enactive Vision

Active Perception Through Touch

The enactive approach to cognition posits that cognition is a dynamic coupling between an agent and it's environment. Inspired by the principles of enaction, this VR project explores an alternative way of perception, or vision, through virtual touch.

Users are placed in a seemingly blank virtual world, where only a few drifting particles offer clues to the hidden space around them. As they reach out and make contact with these particles, forms begin to emerge — the invisible becomes visible through touch. Surfaces reveal themselves not all at once, but through intentional action: a brush of the hand uncovers a table, a step forward gives shape to the floor.

As the environment gradually becomes more visible, it also shapes and is shaped by the user's behavior, highlighting the reciprocal nature of perception and action.

Enactive Vision was developed as part of a group project, with the core concept and full implementation carried out by me. I conceived the central idea — an environment that reveals itself through touch, inspired by the enactive approach to cognition — and translated it into an immersive VR experience using Unity. All interaction mechanics, particle-based surface revelation, and system logic were designed and implemented by me. The particle effects, which form the perceptual medium of the experience, were created using Unity’s Visual Effect Graph.

The environmental theme — an abstracted underwater shipwreck — was contributed by my collaborator, providing a conceptual backdrop to the experience. This setting further enhances the feeling of disorientation and gradual discovery, aligning with the project’s exploration of perception as a dynamic, embodied process.

Garden of Death

Without the Dead, This Garden Would Die

The Garden of Death is a data-driven art installation that visualizes the ephemeral nature of real-time supercomputer processes. Visitors enter the garden — an immersive space where the otherwise invisible life cycles of computational tasks are rendered into an ever-shifting, contemplative landscape.

At the heart of the installation is a fluid, continuous visualization of live data streamed from the computation clusters of TU Dresden’s ZIH (Center for Information Services and High Performance Computing). A rope stretched in front of the display acts as an interactive interface; by gently pulling or releasing it, visitors can scroll through clusters and reveal further layers of computational activity.

What distinguishes The Garden of Death is its treatment of completed processes. Rather than vanishing, these "dead" processes begin to wander through the background — drifting like ghosts in a liquid-like flow field. As they move, they inject subtle streams of color into the space, their trails slowly mixing into the surrounding environment.

"The Garden of Death" by Hugo Simberg

The palette used for this diffusion is directly drawn from Hugo Simberg’s painting The Garden of Death, which inspired the installation's conceptual framework. Without these lingering traces, the background would gradually fade to black, in other words, die — a visual metaphor for a world without memory, devoid of past lives.

This project was developed as part of the Data Art course at TU Dresden. I conceived and executed the entire concept — from the overall installation design to the real-time digital visualization — and implemented the system using openFrameworks in C++. The liquid-like background was developed through custom GLSL shader programming to create a richly textured visual substrate.

Early concept sketches I created — including one illustrating the idea of a physical rope-based interaction — are shown above.

I also tackled the core challenge of representing real-time processes with highly variable lifespans and volume by designing a system that scales gracefully — clustering active processes and giving continued presence to completed ones. My collaborator contributed by building and installing the physical rope interface and implementing the sensor system using Arduino to detect tension and translate it into input for the visualization.

Illustrations

Hand Drawn Illustrations

As part of my work at the Immersive Experience Lab (ixlab) at TU Dresden, I have created a wide range of illustrations to support and visually articulate the lab's research and projects. These illustrations have been featured in academic papers, presentations, and live demonstrations, where they play a key role in communicating complex concepts and showcasing the lab’s capabilities to diverse audiences.

My illustration process spans both traditional and digital methods. I work with hand-drawn sketches as well as digital raster and vector techniques — often combining the two to achieve a particular aesthetic or communicative clarity. For digital work, I primarily use Krita and GIMP for raster-based painting, and Inkscape for vector-based illustrations, depending on the needs of the project.

Portfolio

A new Challenge

To build my personal portfolio, I set myself the challenge of learning web development and creating the entire site from the ground up. I developed the website using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, and incorporated Tailwind CSS for responsive styling.

A key feature of the site is the 3D hero scene, which I designed and modeled entirely from scratch (exluding the human and door models) in Blender, including texturing. I integrated this scene into the website using Three.js, allowing for a dynamic and visually engaging introduction that reflects my interest in both visual design and interactive media.
